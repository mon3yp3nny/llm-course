Step 1

It starts with text

Everything a large language model (LLM) does begins with a string of characters: the prompt. The model never sees your intent, only this text. Change it and watch every step below change with it.

0 characters

From step 2 on, three small meters under each heading show roughly what the step demands of the hardware. CPU, the central processing unit, is the ordinary processor found in every computer. GPU, a graphics processing unit, is a chip built to do huge numbers of simple calculations at once, which is exactly what a neural network needs. Memory is how much has to be loaded at once; here it means the computer's working storage, not remembering, which the FAQ covers. The CPU and GPU meters show how much work a step is compared with the other steps in the same tab.

Optional System prompt: the text you do not see

In a chat product, your prompt is not the only text the model receives. The company running it puts its own instructions in front: the system prompt. It sets things like the tone, the rules and today's date. Everything is then joined into one long text, with special marker tokens that say who is speaking.

[system] You are a helpful assistant. Answer briefly and politely. [user] [assistant]

The model continues this text after the last marker.

The impact: the same model can behave very differently in two products, because their instructions differ. A system prompt changes no weights. It is simply more text at the start of the input, and it uses up part of the context window.

Simplified: the markers look different for every model, and real system prompts are often several pages long. The rest of this page continues with your prompt alone.

Step 2

The text is cut into tokens

Simple text processing. Any processor does it in an instant.

A model cannot read letters or words directly. It works with tokens: common chunks of text. Short, frequent words are usually one token; rarer words are split into pieces. The space before a word belongs to the token (shown here as ·).

0 tokens from 0 characters

Simplified: real tokenizers (such as BPE) learn their pieces from huge amounts of text. This demo only cuts off common endings such as -ing or -ization, so it splits some everyday words and leaves some rare ones whole. A real tokenizer would do the opposite.

Origins Who came up with tokens?
  • 1994Philip Gage publishes byte pair encoding (BPE), a way to compress data by merging pairs of characters that often occur together.
  • 2015Rico Sennrich, Barry Haddow and Alexandra Birch at the University of Edinburgh use it to split rare words into pieces for machine translation. Paper
  • 2019GPT-2 from OpenAI uses this kind of tokenizer. Most LLMs since use it or a close relative.

Step 3

Each token becomes a number

A lookup in a list.

The model has a fixed vocabulary, a list of every token it knows, often 50,000 to 200,000 entries. Each token is replaced by its position in that list. From here on, your prompt is just a sequence of integers. The number under each token is its ID.

        Optional Multimodal: images as input
        

          Text is not the only possible input. A multimodal model
          also takes images, and sometimes sound or video. The trick is to
          turn them into the same thing text becomes: a sequence of vectors.
          An image is cut into small squares called patches, and each patch
          is converted into one vector, just as each token is. Click a patch.
        

        

          

          

          

        

        

          From here on, the layers treat patch vectors and token vectors
          alike, and attention lets a word look at the parts of the picture
          it refers to. The impact: one model can describe a photo, read a
          chart or work from a screenshot. The price: a picture becomes a
          long row of vectors, so a single image can use as much of the
          context window as several pages of text.
        

        

          Simplified: real models cut an image into hundreds of patches, and
          the vector shown here is made up.

Step 5

      
The vectors enter the neural network

      
The heavy part. All the weights must be loaded into GPU memory, and every layer means billions of multiplications.

      

        This is the part people mean when they say "the model". The
        neural network is a long stack of layers. The
        vectors go in at one end, and every layer rewrites them a little,
        mixing in more of the surrounding context each time. The design of
        this stack used by today's LLMs is called a transformer. It is
        the T in GPT: generative pre-trained transformer.
      

      

        Follow the token you selected in step 4 through the stack. Click a
        layer to see its vector at that point.
      

      

        

        
EmbeddingOutput

        

        

      

      

        What each layer does to a vector is decided by its
        weights, also called parameters: plain numbers,
        billions of them in a large model. They are found during
        training: the model is shown enormous amounts of text,
        asked again and again to guess the next token, and its weights are
        nudged a little each time so that the guess gets better. The
        second tab, Training a model, shows how. After that they are frozen. Using the finished model is called
        inference: when you chat, your prompt changes the vectors
        flowing through, never the weights. Training a finished model a
        little further on specialised text is called fine-tuning.
      

      

        What does the inside of a layer look like? Zoom in and you find
        neurons: tiny units that each do one small calculation. A
        neuron takes the numbers arriving on its lines, multiplies each by
        that line's weight, adds everything up and passes on the result.
        Below, the vector of the token you selected in step 4 flows through
        a miniature network, from left to right.

        Optional Other chips: TPU, NPU, and why not quantum
        

          The GPU is the most common chip for neural networks, but not the
          only one. A TPU, tensor processing unit, is Google's own
          chip, designed from the start for the calculations of neural
          networks; Google trains and runs its Gemini models on them, and
          other large companies build similar chips of their own. An
          NPU, neural processing unit, is the small relative found
          in recent phones and laptops, built to run modest models on the
          device itself with little power.
        

        

          All of these do the job this guide ascribes to the GPU: huge
          numbers of simple calculations at once. They give up some of the
          GPU's flexibility to be cheaper or more economical at that one
          job. Where a meter on this page says GPU, read it as "a chip of
          this kind". The Hardware tab compares
          whole machines.
        

        

          Apple calls the NPU in its phones and Macs the
          Neural Engine. It may come as a surprise that the usual
          tools for running language models on a Mac do not use it. It was
          built for tasks of a fixed size, such as recognising images and
          speech, and writing text token by token suits it poorly. On a Mac,
          models run on the graphics part of the chip, helped by fast memory
          that the processor and the graphics share.
        

        

          "AI GPU" is a label with three meanings. It can mean a graphics
          chip with extra circuits built in for the arithmetic of neural
          networks, which most recent ones have. It can mean the cards in
          data centres, which are GPUs in name only: they have no connection
          for a screen and a great deal of memory, and they are what the big
          models run on. And it can mean the reverse, a graphics card that
          uses a small neural network to sharpen the picture in games. For
          running a model at home, none of these labels is the number that
          matters. That is the card's memory, called VRAM, because
          it decides which models fit.
        

        

          A QPU, quantum processing unit, is something else
          entirely, and it plays no part in today's language models. No LLM
          is trained or run on a quantum computer. The machines that exist
          are far too small and too error-prone for billions of weights, and
          no advantage for this kind of calculation has been shown. There is
          research, mostly small experiments in which a tiny part of a model
          runs on a quantum chip. For now the help flows mainly the other
          way: AI is used to help quantum computers correct their errors.
        

      

      

        Optional Quantization: making a model smaller
        

          Each weight is normally stored as a very precise number, using 16
          or 32 bits. Quantization stores every weight with fewer
          bits, by rounding it to the nearest of a small set of allowed
          values. Choose how many bits each weight gets.
        

        

          

          
Eight example weights, as trained

          

          

          

          

          
Size of a model with 7 billion parameters

          

        

        

          The impact: fewer bits mean a smaller file, less memory and faster
          answers, because there is less data to move for every token. This is what lets a model run on a laptop or a phone
          instead of in a data centre. The price is a small rounding error
          in every weight. With the rounding methods in everyday use, 8 bits is
          barely measurable, around 4 bits answers get slightly worse, and
          below that quality drops quickly. Newer methods get much lower
          without that drop; see the examples below.
        

        

          Simplified: real quantization picks the allowed values more
          cleverly, separately for each group of weights. Labels such as
          "4-bit" or "Q4" on a downloadable model refer to this.

        Optional Test-time training: learning while answering
        

          The rule above was: once training is over, the weights are frozen.
          Test-time training (TTT) is the exception. Before it
          answers, the model runs a few quick training steps on the task in
          front of it, for example on the examples in your prompt, so its
          weights shift towards this one problem. After the answer the
          changes are usually thrown away, and the next request starts from
          the original weights again.
        

        

          

          

          

            
            
          

        

        

          The impact: the model copes better with unfamiliar tasks that
          differ from anything in its training text, such as new kinds of
          puzzles. The price: every request now contains a small training
          run, which makes it much slower and more expensive. So far it is
          mostly used in research, not in everyday chat products.
        

        

          Two things it is easy to confuse it with. Fine-tuning also changes
          the weights, but ahead of time and for good. And a prompt that
          simply contains examples changes no weights at all.
        

        

          Simplified: the changes to the weights here are made up.

        Optional Open weights: models you can download
        

          A trained model is, in the end, a file full of weights. Some
          companies keep that file to themselves and only let you send
          prompts to their servers. Others publish it. A model with
          open weights can be downloaded and run on your own
          hardware, and it is what makes quantization and fine-tuning by
          anyone possible in the first place.
        

        

          The impact: your prompts never leave your machine, there is no
          price per token, the model keeps working offline, and nobody can
          change or withdraw it. The price: you need the hardware and the
          know-how, and the very largest models are usually not released
          this way.
        

        

          Open weights are not the same as open source. The file is public,
          but the training text and training code often are not, and the
          licence decides what you may do with the model.

Step 6

      
Tokens look at each other

      
Every token is compared with every earlier one, so the work grows quickly as the text gets longer.

      

        A token on its own is ambiguous: "mat" means something different after
        "sat on the" than after "yoga". So what happens inside a layer? The
        key part: each token looks back at the tokens before it and pulls in
        information from the ones that matter. This is
        attention.
      

      

        Click a token in this example sentence. The shading shows how much
        it draws from itself and from each earlier token. Start with "it": to know what "it"
        is, the model has to look back at "cat". A token can never look
        ahead, so later tokens are hidden from it.
      

      

        

        

      

      

        Simplified: these percentages are written by hand. They show the kind of
        pattern researchers find inside trained models, such as a pronoun
        looking back at the noun it stands for.
      

      

        Where do the percentages come from? The model compares the vector of
        the current token with its own vector and with that of each earlier token, and turns
        the scores into shares that add up to 100%. Here is that calculation
        on your own prompt.
      

      

        

        

      

      

        Simplified: the calculation (compare, then turn the scores into
        shares) is real, but a real model first reshapes each vector with
        learned weights, and the vectors here are made up, so this second
        pattern has no meaning. A trained model runs many such patterns
        side by side, called attention heads, in every one of
        its layers. Together they pick up grammar, references and facts.
      

      

        Optional Mixture of experts: using only part of the model
        

          Attention is one half of a layer. The other half works on each
          token separately and holds most of the layer's weights. In a
          mixture of experts (MoE) model, that half is split into
          several smaller blocks, the "experts". A small router looks at
          each token and sends it to only a few of them. Click a token to
          see where it goes.
        

        

          

          

          

        

        

          The impact: the model can hold far more weights, and so more
          knowledge, than it uses for any single token. That makes it faster
          and cheaper to run than an ordinary model of the same size. The
          price: all experts still have to fit in memory, and such models
          are harder to train well.
        

        

          The name misleads a little. Experts are not tidy specialists for
          maths or poetry. What each one handles is learned during training
          and is usually hard to put into words.
        

        

          Simplified: the routing here is made up. For scale: Mixtral 8x7B
          (2023) has 8 experts per layer and uses 2 per token, so of its
          roughly 47 billion parameters only about 13 billion are at work
          for each token.

Step 7

      
A score for every possible next token

      
One more comparison, against the whole vocabulary. Small next to the layers.

      

        After all the layers, the vector of the last token holds the
        model's summary of the whole prompt. It is compared against every
        token in the vocabulary, giving each one a probability: how likely
        is it to come next?
      

      

        

        

        

      

      

        Notice what is being scored: how likely a token is, not
        whether it is true. When a model produces fluent, confident text
        that is wrong, people call it a hallucination.
      

      

        Simplified: these numbers do not come from the vectors and layers
        above. They come from a much simpler kind of model, which only
        counts which token followed which in about 60 short sentences about
        cats, dogs and tokens. It knows about 100 tokens and looks only at
        the last two tokens of your prompt. A real LLM produces the same
        kind of list using the layers from steps 5 and 6.
      

      

        Optional RAG: looking things up first
        

          A model only knows what was in its training text. Anything newer,
          or private, such as your company's documents, it cannot know, and
          it may hallucinate an answer instead.

          does not change the model. It changes the input: before the model
          runs, a search step finds passages that fit your prompt and pastes
          them in front of it. The model then answers with the facts right
          there in its context window.

Step 8

      
One token is picked

      
Drawing one random number. Practically free.

      

        The software running the model does not simply take the top token. It holds a lottery:
        a token with 42% holds 42 of 100 tickets, and one ticket is drawn.
        Likely tokens usually win, but an unlikely one sometimes does. This
        weighted random draw is called sampling.
      

      

        Temperature hands out the tickets differently before the
        draw: low values make the favourite almost certain, high values give
        unlikely tokens a real chance. After the draw the probabilities are
        thrown away. Only the winning token is kept.
      

      

        
        
        

        

        

          
          
        

        

      

      

        Pick several times: this randomness is why the same prompt can give
        different answers. At temperature 0 the top token always wins.
      

      

        Origins Where do temperature and sampling come from?
        
    
          
  • 1800sThe word temperature is borrowed from physics. Ludwig Boltzmann described how heat spreads particles over more states; the same formula spreads the probabilities here.
    • 
          
  • 2019Ari Holtzman and colleagues at the University of Washington introduce nucleus sampling, better known as top-p. You will find it next to temperature in many tools. Paper

Step 9

      
Repeat until done

      
Everything again for every new token. Results for earlier tokens are kept in memory so they need not be recomputed, and that store grows with the text. For each new token the chip reads through all the weights, so how fast it can pull data out of memory, the memory bandwidth, often limits the speed more than raw calculating power.

      

        One token is not an answer. The picked token is glued to the end of
        the input, and everything runs again: tokens, vectors, attention,
        probabilities, pick. An LLM writes its whole answer this way, one
        token at a time, each one a fresh pass through the model.
      

      

        The text keeps growing, and there is a limit to how many tokens the
        model can take in at once: its context window. In a
        chat, the whole conversation so far is fed in again for every new
        answer. That is the only way the model "remembers" what was said;
        whatever no longer fits in the window is gone.
      

      

        

        

          
          
          
        

      

      

        Simplified: a real model stops when it picks a special "end" token.
        This demo stops at a full stop once it has written at least 8
        tokens, or after 40 at the latest. It uses the temperature from
        step 8, and its text sounds like its 63 training sentences because
        that is all this tiny model has ever read. In practice a model also
        keeps its intermediate results for earlier tokens, so each new pass
        only has to work through the one new token.
      

      

        Optional Input and output tokens: what an answer costs
        

          Tokens are also the unit by which use of a model is billed, at
          least for developers and companies; chat subscriptions hide this
          behind a flat fee with usage limits. There are two kinds. Input tokens are everything the model reads: the
          system prompt, the conversation so far, attached documents, tool
          descriptions. Output tokens are what it writes.
        

        

          Reading is cheap: all input tokens pass through the layers
          together, in one go. Writing is expensive: every output token
          needs a pass of its own, which is the loop of this step. That is
          why output is slower, and usually priced several times higher per
          token than input.
        

        

          
          
          

          

        

        

          In a long chat the whole history is read again on every turn, so
          the input side grows much faster than the output side. The bars
          marked "Cached" and "Compacted" show the two remedies.
        

        

          Prompt caching: the provider keeps its intermediate
          results for the beginning of the input it has just processed. When
          the next request starts with the same text, that part is not
          worked through again, and it is billed at a fraction of the price,
          often about a tenth. Caching does not remove a single token. The
          text still fills the context window, and one changed word near
          the start makes everything after it count as new. The cache is kept only for some minutes. It saves money and waiting time, not room.
        

        

          Compaction: when a conversation approaches the limit of
          the context window, the software around the model has the older
          part summarised and carries on with the summary in its place. This
          does remove tokens, so it saves room as well as money. The price
          is that details from the summarised part are gone, which is why an
          assistant in a very long session can forget something you said
          early on.
        

        

          Simplified: fixed sizes are assumed, a system prompt of 300
          tokens, questions of 30 and answers of 200. Cached input is
          counted at a tenth. Compaction replaces the earlier turns with a
          summary of 300 tokens whenever they exceed 2,000, and the cost of writing that summary is
          left out. The bars count tokens, not money: output tokens cost
          several times more each, so with caching the output is often the
          larger part of the bill.
        

      

      

        Optional Reasoning models: thinking before answering
        

          One pass through the layers can only work out so much, and
          nothing carries over to the next pass except the text itself. So
          for longer problems, the text is the model's only scratch paper. A reasoning model uses the loop
          for exactly that: before the answer, it first writes out its
          working, step by step, as ordinary tokens. Many products hide this
          part or show it as "thinking". Then it writes the answer, with its
          own working sitting in the context window to draw on.
        

        

          
[user] What is 17 × 24?
[thinking] 17 × 24 is 17 × 20 plus 17 × 4. That is 340 plus 68, so 408.
[assistant] 408

          
The thinking is generated token by token, exactly like the answer.

        

        

          The impact: clearly better results on problems with several steps,
          such as maths, planning and programming. The price: the thinking
          tokens take time and cost money like any others, so answers are
          slower and more expensive. And it is still the same loop: the
          working can contain mistakes too.
        

        

          Simplified: this exchange is written by hand as an illustration.
          The tiny model on this page cannot calculate.

        Optional Agents: letting the model use tools
        

          A model can only write text. It cannot look anything up, calculate
          reliably or press a button. An agent gets around this
          with tool use: the model is told which tools exist, and
          when it wants one it writes a request in a fixed format. Ordinary
          software notices the request, runs the tool, and pastes the result
          back into the text. Then the loop simply continues. Step through
          one round here; the Agents tab shows
          what is built around it.
        

        

          

          

          

            
            
          

        

        

          The impact: the model can work with current information, exact
          calculations and real actions, such as searching the web, running
          code or sending a message, and it can chain many such rounds to
          finish a longer task. The price: every round costs time and
          tokens, and a mistake is no longer just a wrong sentence. It can
          be a wrong action, which is why agents need limits on what they
          may do.

          since nothing is being written, such a model can let every token

          answers on offer. Nothing is sampled and nothing loops: the

How an LLM learns what it knows

Training · Step 1

      
It starts with text, a lot of it

      
Collecting, cleaning and tokenising is ordinary computing, but at the scale of a large part of the internet. What it needs most is storage.

      

        Before any learning, someone has to collect text: web pages, books,
        articles, program code, conversations. The
        training data of a large model runs to trillions of words,
        more than a person could read in a thousand lifetimes. It is
        cleaned, filtered and cut into tokens, exactly as in
        step 2 of the first tab.
      

      

        What goes in matters. A model picks up the knowledge in its data,
        and also its mistakes, its biases and its style.
      

      

        Collecting stops on some date. Whatever happened afterwards is not
        in the data, which is why every model has a
        knowledge cutoff: it knows nothing more recent unless
        the text is handed to it in the prompt.
      

    


    

      
Training · Step 2

      
Learning by guessing the next token

      
The most expensive step of all: thousands of GPUs for weeks or months.

      

        The first and by far the largest phase is pre-training.
        The exercise is always the same: take a piece of real text, hide
        what comes next, let the model guess, then reveal the answer. Nobody
        has to label anything, because the text itself contains the right
        answers. Repeated over trillions of tokens, this one exercise forces
        the model to pick up spelling, grammar, facts and some reasoning,
        since all of them help it guess better.
      

      

        How wrong a guess was is measured by one number, the
        loss: high when the real next token came as a surprise,
        low when the model saw it coming. Training means making the loss go
        down. Let this page's tiny model read its sentences, and watch.

        What comes out of pre-training is called a base model: very
        good at continuing text, and nothing more yet. Pre-training a large
        one keeps thousands of specialised chips (GPUs) busy for weeks or
        months, which is why only a few organisations do it.
      

      

        Simplified: this tiny model learns by counting what followed what,

Training · Step 3

      
How the weights are nudged

      
Training needs more memory than use: the intermediate results of every layer are kept to work backwards from, plus a nudge for every single weight.

      

        A real model keeps no tally of what followed what. It starts with random weights and guesses
        nonsense. After each guess, a method called
        backpropagation works out, for every single weight,
        whether turning it up or down a little would have made the loss
        smaller. Then all weights are moved a small step in the helpful
        direction. This is gradient descent: walking downhill on
        the loss, one small step at a time, with billions of weights at once.
      

      

        How far each step goes depends on two things: how steep the slope is,
        and a setting called the learning rate that scales every
        step. Too low and learning crawls. Too high and each step jumps over
        the valley and lands higher than before. Try it on a
        model that has just one weight. The curve shows the loss for every
        possible value of that weight; the best value is at the bottom.
      

      

        

        
        

        

          
          
        

      

      

        Simplified: one weight and one smooth valley. A real model has
        billions of weights and a far more rugged landscape, but every step
        works this way.

Training · Step 4

      
From text machine to assistant

      
The same machinery on far less data: days, not months. The whole model still has to be loaded.

      

        A base model does not answer questions. It continues text, and a
        question is often continued with more questions. Everything that
        turns it into a helpful assistant is called
        post-training.
      

      

        The first part is fine-tuning: continuing training on a
        much smaller, carefully chosen set of examples. Here the examples
        are written conversations, used with the same next-token exercise,
        in which an assistant
        follows instructions, answers clearly and declines harmful requests.
        This is also where it learns the conversation format with its
        markers, shown under "System prompt" in
        step 1 of the first tab.

        typical difference.
      

    


    

      
Training · Step 5

      
Learning from preferences

      
Plus the scarcest resource in this step: the time of the people who judge the answers.

      

        Good answers are hard to write down in advance, but easy to
        recognise. So the next part of post-training works with judgements:
        the model produces several answers, and people, or other models,
        say which is better. This is reinforcement learning: instead of
        being shown the right answer, as in step 4, the model gets only a
        score for what it produced, and its weights are nudged so that
        higher-scoring answers become more likely. When the judgements come from people, this is known as
        RLHF, reinforcement learning from human feedback. Be the
        judge.
      

      

        

        

        

        

          
        

      

      

        Much of a model's character comes from this phase: how helpful,
        honest, careful or chatty it is. Getting a model to reliably behave
        as intended is called alignment. There is a risk on the
        other side: a model trained to please its judges can learn to sound
        convincing or flattering instead of being right.
      

      

        Simplified: your clicks are only recorded here and train nothing. In real training,
        many thousands of such comparisons are first used to train a second
        model that scores answers, and that score then steers the training.

Training · Step 6

      
Testing before release

      
Running the finished model on thousands of test questions. No learning, but the whole model has to be loaded.

      

        How do you know whether training worked? The loss only says how well
        the model predicts text. So models are also tested on tasks. A
        benchmark is a fixed set of questions with known answers,
        such as exam questions, programming tasks or puzzles, and the score
        is the share the model gets right. One rule matters above all: the
        test must not be part of the training data, or the model may simply
        have memorised the answers.
      

      

        The tiny model shows why. Here is its loss, after reading all its
        sentences, on three of those sentences and on three new ones built
        from the same words. Lower is better.
      

      

        

        

      

      

        Only the second number says how the model copes with text it meets
        for the first time. Testing on what it has already read would make
        it look far better than it is.
      

      

        Benchmark scores are how models are compared in headlines, and they
        have limits. A high score in a test is not the same as being useful
        or safe in real work. So before a release, developers also have
        people try on purpose to make the model misbehave, which is called
        red teaming.
      

    


    

      
Training · Step 7

      
Adapting a finished model

      
LoRA trains only a small add-on, so one graphics card can be enough, though the whole frozen model must still be loaded. Distillation is a larger job.

      

        Once a model exists, others can keep shaping it without starting
        over.
      

      

        Fine-tuning trains it a little further on your own
        examples, to teach it a style, a format or a specialist field.
      

      

        LoRA is a cheap way to do that. The original weights stay
        frozen, and only an
        add-on with a small fraction of the weights is trained. That
        brings fine-tuning within reach of a single graphics card. The
        add-on is two thin tables whose product has the same shape as the
        big one, and its result is added to what the frozen table produces.
        How thin they are is called the rank.
      

      

        
One table of weights inside a layer, 4,096 by 4,096 numbers. Choose the size of the add-on.

        

        

        

      

      

        Simplified: one table is shown. A real model has hundreds, and an
        add-on is attached to many of them. Typical ranks are 4 to 64; 256
        is included to show where the saving shrinks. The counts are exact
        for a table of this size.
      

      

        Distillation trains a small "student" model to imitate the
        answers of a large "teacher" model. The result is smaller and
        faster, and keeps much of the teacher's ability.
      

      

        Much of what looks like teaching a model is not training at all. A
        system prompt, retrieved documents and tools all change the input,
        not the weights. They are cheaper, take effect at once and can be
        undone, so they are usually the first thing to try. Quantization is a different
        case: it does change the weights, but by rounding them, not by
        training, so the model gets smaller without learning anything new.
        All of these are in the first tab.

Agents · Step 1

      
A goal instead of a question

      

        A chatbot takes one prompt and gives one answer.
        Agentic AI describes systems that are given a goal and
        work towards it over many rounds: decide on an action, carry it out
        with a tool, look at the result, decide again. The model supplies
        the decisions. Everything else is ordinary software. A single such
        round is shown under "Agents" in
        step 9 of the first tab.
      

      

        "Agentic" is a matter of degree, not a yes or no. Compare three
        levels.
      

      

        The first is a chatbot: you decide after every single answer, and it
        touches nothing, it only writes text. The second is an assistant with
        tools: the model decides for a few rounds, using the tools you have
        switched on, and you approve anything important. The third is an
        autonomous agent: the model decides for hundreds of rounds and works
        with files, programs and online services, within its permissions.

        costly. The skill lies in granting as much as the task needs, and
        no more.
      

    


    

      
Agents · Step 2

      
The harness: everything around the model

      

        The program that turns a model into an agent is called the
        harness. It runs the loop: it assembles the input, calls
        the model, reads what the model wrote, carries out tool requests,
        enforces the rules, and stops when the model gives a plain answer or a limit is reached. Walk once around the
        loop.
      

      

        

        

        

          
          
        

      

      

        Four of the five stations belong to the harness. That is why the
        same model can feel like a different product in two places: what it
        is told, which tools it gets, what it may do without asking and how
        its context window is managed are all decided here. The harness
        itself is ordinary software and runs on any CPU. Only the model
        calls need GPUs.
      

      

        Simplified: real harnesses add more, such as running several tool
        requests at once, retrying after errors and handing parts of a task
        to further agents.
      

    


    

      
Agents · Step 3

      
Several tools, one task

      

        Real tasks rarely need just one tool. In a tool chain,
        the result of one tool becomes the input of the next, and the model
        decides after each result what to do with it. Step through a chain
        of three.
      

      

        

        

        

          
          
        

      

      

        A chain is only as strong as its links. If each step goes right 95% of the time and no mistake is caught, a task of 10 steps succeeds about 60% of the time, and
        one of 50 steps about 8%. This is why long tasks are hard for
        agents, and why harnesses add checks, tests and retries along the
        way.
      

      

        Simplified: the model's lines are written by hand. The three tool
        results are computed for real by this page, including today's date.
      

    


    

      
Agents · Step 4

      
MCP: one plug for all tools

      

        Every tool has to be connected to the harness somehow. At first each
        product wrote its own connection for each tool. MCP, the
        Model Context Protocol, is an open standard for it. The maker of a
        tool writes one small program, an MCP server, which announces which tools it offers and how to call them, and carries out the calls the harness sends it. Any harness that speaks MCP
        can connect to it, and passes those descriptions on to the model.
        It is often compared to a universal plug such as USB-C.
      

      

        A server can offer three kinds of things: tools, which are actions
        the model may request; resources, which are data it may read, such
        as files or records; and ready-made prompts for common tasks. It
        can run as a small program on your own computer, or as a service on
        the internet that you log in to. Either way the first exchange is
        the same: the harness asks "what do you offer?", and the server
        answers with a list, each entry with a description in plain language and the exact inputs it expects.
      

      

        Connect and disconnect servers, and watch what the model is told.
      

      

        

        

        

      

      

        The descriptions are input like any other: the model reads them on every round. So connecting everything is not free, and a
        long list of tools also makes it harder for the model to pick the
        right one. And an MCP server is somebody else's software that your
        agent will act through, so it deserves the same care as any program
        you install.
      

      
MCP, an API or the command line?

      

        There were ways to connect software long before MCP. An
        API is the interface through which one program calls
        another over the network. A CLI, the command line, is the
        text interface in which programs are started by typing commands. An
        agent can use all three, and they differ in ways that matter.
      

      

        Put side by side, the three compare like this. An API is made for
        programmers, the command line for people typing
        commands, and MCP for models. With an API, somebody has to describe
        each function to the model. Command-line programs the model often
        knows from its training. An MCP server announces its tools by itself.
        An API exchanges exact, structured data. MCP is just as exact about
        what goes in, and its results come back in a fixed envelope, though
        often as plain text inside it. The command line returns free text
        laid out for human eyes. Permissions differ too: an
        access key or a user account opens everything it covers, whereas MCP
        can grant access tool by tool. And the cost in the context window
        differs: a command costs nothing until it is run, while the
        descriptions of every connected MCP tool are read on each round.

        a plain API come from. A tool is connected once and then works in
        every harness; the model discovers by itself what is on offer; and
        access can be limited tool by tool.
      

      

        A picture may help. An API is a form pushed through a slot: every
        field must be right, or it comes back with an error, and the rules
        are in a manual somewhere else. An MCP tool is the same form, just
        as strict, but at a counter with a clerk. The clerk has the rules
        to hand, fills the form in from what you say in your own words,
        and if it is rejected, reads the reason and corrects it. The clerk
        is the model. That is why MCP feels more like a conversation,
        although every single request is as exact as before. A server can
        even ask a question back when a detail is missing.
      

      

        Compared with the command line, MCP is tidier and safer: exact
        inputs, and results in a fixed envelope instead of whatever a
        program happens to print, permission for
        single actions instead of a whole computer, and it works where there
        is no command line at all, such as a chat app on a phone. The
        command line keeps real strengths, though. It needs no setup, models
        already know the common programs, commands can be combined freely,
        and nothing occupies the context window until it is used. Many
        coding agents therefore use both: the command line for what is
        already installed, MCP for services that need a login or a clear
        boundary.

Agents · Step 5

      
Keeping an agent in bounds

      

        To a model, everything is text: your instructions, a web page it
        reads, the result of a tool. A page or an email can contain a
        sentence written to look like an instruction, and the model may
        follow it. This is called prompt injection. In a chatbot
        the damage is a wrong sentence. In an agent with tools it can be a
        wrong action.
      

      

        Models are trained to resist this, but none does so reliably, and
        there is no complete fix. So the dependable defences sit in the
        harness: permissions that
        say what the agent may do without asking, a person confirming
        anything that cannot be undone, a closed-off environment to work
        in, and a record of every step.
      

    


    

      That is an agent: a model in a loop, a harness around it, tools within
      reach, and limits on all of it.
    

    


    

    

      
What it runs on

      

        The same model can crawl on one machine and fly on another. This
        part compares the kinds of computer a model can run on, and explains
        the difference.

Hardware · Step 1

      
Three numbers decide everything

      

        Running a model makes three demands on a machine, and almost every
        difference between computers comes down to them.
      

      

        The first is memory. All the weights have to be loaded, so the model
        must fit. The second is memory bandwidth, called reading speed in the rest of
        this part: how many gigabytes of weights the chip can pull out of
        memory per second. For every new token the weights are read through
        once, so that speed sets the pace of writing. The third is computing power, the number of
        calculations the chip can do per second. It matters most when a long
        prompt is read in, and during training.
      

      

        No machine is best at all three. Each kind of computer in this part
        is a different compromise.
      

    


    

      
Hardware · Step 2

      
A computer without a strong graphics chip

      

        An office laptop or desktop has a CPU and ordinary memory. There is
        often plenty of that memory, and it is cheap. But it is slow to
        read, and a CPU has a handful of processing cores where a graphics
        chip has thousands. So a small model works, and you can watch it
        write word by word. Large ones fit on paper and are too slow to
        use. An NPU, a small part of the chip for AI tasks in recent laptops, helps
        with small jobs such as turning speech into text, not with a large
        language model.

Hardware · Step 3

      
A PC with a graphics card

      

        A graphics card is a second computer inside the computer: its own chip and its own memory, called VRAM, joined by an extremely fast connection. A model that fits entirely into the VRAM runs many times
        faster than on the CPU. The catch is the word "fits". Cards for home
        use carry 8 to 32 GB, and whatever does not fit has to stay in the
        slow ordinary memory, which drags the whole model down towards that
        speed.
      

      

        Nearly all the cards used for AI come from NVIDIA, and much of its lead is software. CUDA, its platform for programming the chips,
        is what most AI tools were written for first.

Hardware · Step 4

      
A Mac with Apple's own chips

      

        Apple's chips take a different route. Processor, graphics and an extra part for AI tasks, the Neural Engine, sit on one chip and share one pool of fast memory, called
        unified memory. Nothing has to be copied to a separate
        card, and the graphics part can use nearly all of it. A Mac with 64 or 128 GB can therefore load models that no graphics
        card for home use can hold, and the largest Mac Studio offers 512
        GB.
      

      

        Its reading speed spans the two worlds: about that of an ordinary
        PC on the smallest chips, and as fast as a top graphics card on the
        largest. A phone is built the same way in miniature: one chip and
        one shared pool of memory, of which an app may use about half. Its raw
        computing power is lower than a large NVIDIA card's, which shows
        when long prompts are read in, and in training. Others have followed: NVIDIA's DGX Spark is a small desktop box built
        on the same idea.

Hardware · Step 5

      
A server in a data centre

      

        The models behind the big chat services are far too large for any of
        this. They run on servers that hold eight or more data-centre GPUs,
        each with 80 GB of memory or more, of a faster kind than anything
        sold for a desk. A model is spread across several cards, linked by
        connections fast enough for them to work as one.
      

      

        Such a server does not wait on one person. It works on many requests
        at the same moment, which is what makes the price per token low.
        Training uses the same machines by the thousand, wired into one
        cluster that draws as much power as a town. The chips need not come from NVIDIA: Google runs its own models on chips of its own design, called TPUs.

Hardware · Step 6

      
What fits where, and how fast

      

        This step is a table of seven machines, from a phone and a laptop to
        a Mac Studio and a data-centre server. You choose a model and how many
        bits each weight gets. For every machine the table then shows whether
        the model fits, how fast it could write at most, and, in the last
        column, how much it writes for the power it draws.
      

      

        Two rules from this guide are enough for a rough estimate. Size: the
        number of parameters times the bits per weight. Speed: a machine
        cannot write tokens faster than it can read through the weights, so
        its reading speed divided by the size of the model gives an upper
        limit. The last column divides that speed by the power the machine
        draws. The result, tokens per watt, says how much writing
        you get for the electricity. Choose a model and see where it runs.

        at a pace you can read along with, on a few watts. Then try 70B at 4

        home, and it cannot load the model at all; the Macs and the DGX
        Spark can, at a modest pace. At 16 bits the Mac Studio with 512 GB is the only single machine
        here that still holds it; a data-centre server does it by spreading
        the model over several cards. And

        today, Kimi K3 with 2.8 trillion parameters: none of these machines
        comes close. That trade, speed against room, is the whole
        story of running models yourself. The power column adds a third
        view: the fastest machine is not automatically the most economical
        one.
      

      

        Simplified: the speed limit is only reading speed divided by model
        size. Real speeds are lower, often half, and depend on the software.
        A model counts as fitting if it takes at most 85% of the memory,
        leaving room for the conversation. On the laptop the limit is 60%
        and on the phone 50%, because the system and other programs need
        the rest.
        The figures for the machines are typical and rounded, and the power figures are for the machine
        working hard. "Tokens per watt" is strictly tokens per second per
        watt; multiplied by 3,600 it gives tokens per watt-hour. A
        data-centre server looks worse here than it is: it
        answers many people at once, which multiplies what it delivers per
        watt. Kimi K3 is a
        mixture-of-experts model that uses only a small part of its weights
        for each token, so its real speed on a large enough server is far
        better than its size suggests.

When the model gets a body

      

        The same kind of model that writes text is now being taught to move
        machines. This part looks at what robots can do today, how their
        models differ from a chat model, and what to expect next.
      

      

        Snapshot: October 2026. This field moves fast, and its public
        demonstrations run well ahead of everyday reality.
      

      ↓
    


    

      
Robots · Step 1

      
What robots can do today

      

        The robots that already work in large numbers look nothing like
        people: arms welding cars, carts moving shelves in warehouses,
        vacuum cleaners, driverless taxis. Each does one job in a setting
        built for it.
      

      

        The new development is the humanoid, a robot with the
        shape of a person, meant to work in places built for people. Since
        2024 humanoids have moved from stage demonstrations to first real jobs:
        carrying parts in car factories, moving boxes in warehouses,
        sorting parcels for days on end. The first models for the home can
        be ordered, at about the price of a small car.
      

      

        It helps to keep the scale in view. Most humanoids delivered so far
        are used for shows, teaching and collecting training data. Where
        they do real work, a person is still several times faster, and the
        robots cope best where the surroundings are tidy and predictable.
        A cluttered kitchen is far harder than a factory floor.
      

    


    

      
Robots · Step 2

      
How a robot's model differs from a chat model

      

        The model inside the newest robots is called a
        vision-language-action model, VLA for short. It is built
        like an LLM and usually starts from one that already understands
        pictures and text. What changes is what goes in and what comes out.
        In goes the camera picture and an instruction. Out comes not a
        sentence but the next small movement. Step through one task.

        In the simplest designs each movement is written as a token, so
        "predict the next token" becomes "predict the next movement". Others
        add a small extra part that turns the model's output into smooth
        motion. Many use two parts side by side: a slow one that looks at
        the scene and works out what to do, a few times a second, and a
        fast one that steers the motors, a hundred times a second or more.
      

      

        Put side by side, a chat model and the model of a robot differ in six
        ways. A chat model takes in text and sometimes pictures. The model of
        a robot takes in camera pictures, the position of every joint, and an
        instruction. One gives back the next token of text, the other the next
        small movement. With a chat model you can wait a second for the
        answer. A robot must act many times a second, or it stumbles. A chat
        model runs in a data centre, the model of a robot mostly inside the
        robot, because a delay could mean a fall. The chat model learned from
        the text of the internet. The robot has only recordings of robots at
        work, which are scarce. And where the mistake of a chat model is a
        wrong sentence, that of a robot can be a dropped glass, or worse.

Robots · Step 3

      
Where the training data comes from

      

        An LLM can read the internet. A robot cannot, because nobody ever
        wrote down how it feels to fold a towel. The lack of data is the
        main obstacle in the field, and there are three ways around it.
      

      

        The first is teleoperation: a person steers the robot by
        remote control, often wearing a headset and gloves, while every
        picture and movement is recorded. It gives the best data and is
        slow and expensive. The second is simulation: the robot practises
        in a virtual world, millions of times, far faster than real time.
        That works well for walking and balance, less well for handling
        soft or slippery things. The third is video of people doing
        things, which exists in vast amounts but does not show what the
        robot's own motors should do.
      

      

        Most companies combine all three, and start from a model that has
        already read the internet, so that the robot knows what a towel is
        before it ever touches one.

Their own model, or a shared one?

      

        Who trains the model is the main dividing line. Tesla, Figure and
        1X each train their own, for their own body: Tesla adapts the
        system that drives its cars, Figure has Helix, and 1X has a model
        called Redwood. Unitree has published one of its own for anyone

        on Gemini Robotics, and a growing list of builders, among them LG
        and the German company NEURA Robotics, take NVIDIA's GR00T and
        continue its training on their own robot, which is fine-tuning.

        to learn from, so a company with many robots at work holds
        recordings nobody else has, and wants a model fitted to exactly
        that body. A company without them does better to begin with a
        model that has already learned from many robots. Which approach
        wins is open: there is no test yet that runs all these models on
        the same robot.
      

    


    

      
Robots · Step 5

      
What is foreseeable

      

        In the next year or two, expect more of what has just begun: robots
        doing a handful of well-defined jobs in factories and warehouses,
        in hundreds and then thousands, and the first home robots in the
        hands of early buyers, doing simple chores slowly and often with a
        person helping from afar.
      

      

        Beyond that, forecasts differ widely and age fast. In 2024 one
        research firm expected about 10,000 humanoids to be shipped in 2027;
        by its own count, some 13,000 were already shipped in 2025. Another
        firm expects more than 100,000 for 2027. Robots that are useful in an ordinary home, at
        an ordinary price, are mostly expected around 2030 or later.
      

      

        What holds things back is not the idea but the practice. Hands
        that match a person's dexterity. Working for a whole shift without
        a fault. Being safe next to people. Cost. And, above all, data.
        Language models got good when they had read enough. Robots have
        only just started reading.

Why Europe wants a model of its own

        or China. If you use one through its maker's service, your prompts
        are processed on that company's computers and fall under the law
        of its country. The wish to depend on neither is called
        sovereign AI: a model that is built at home, runs in
        data centres at home, and stays under your own law.

        in its own data centres near Paris, is building another in
        Sweden, and offers a region in which data stays under the law of
        the European Union. Because it also publishes weights, a
        government, a hospital or a bank can instead run the model
        entirely on its own machines.

        downloaded model sends nothing anywhere. What sovereignty adds is
        that the model keeps being developed, and can be served at scale,
        without depending on another country. One dependence remains:
        the chips are still designed in the United States.

Timeline · 1843 to 1956

      
Ideas before the machines

      

        Long before there was a computer that could learn, people argued about whether a machine could think, and worked out the mathematics it would need.
      

      

        Chapter one: ideas before the machines. In 1843 Ada Lovelace translated a paper about a computer
        that Charles Babbage was planning, and added notes three times as long
        as the paper itself. She saw further than the inventor. The machine,
        she wrote, could work on any symbols, not only numbers, and might one
        day compose music. Yet she insisted that it could only ever do what it
        was told. Whether a machine can create something new, or only
        recombine what it was given, is still argued about today. A hundred
        years passed before the next step. In 1943 Warren McCulloch and Walter
        Pitts described a nerve cell as a small piece of mathematics. Every
        neural network descends from that idea. Five years later Claude
        Shannon produced text that looked like English, by choosing each next
        word according to how often it follows the ones before. That, in
        miniature, is what a language model still does. In 1950 Alan Turing
        asked: can machines think? He found the question too vague to answer,
        so he replaced it with a game. A judge exchanges written messages with
        a person and with a machine, without knowing which is which. If the
        judge cannot tell them apart, why deny that the machine thinks?
        Today's chat models can pass versions of his test, and the debate has
        moved on to whether passing it proves anything. And in the summer of
        1956 a handful of researchers met at Dartmouth College. They expected
        to make a significant advance in two months. They did not. But the
        name they had chosen for their project stuck: artificial intelligence.
        So did the habit of announcing that the breakthrough was only a few
        years away.

Timeline · 1958 to 1999

      
First steps and long winters

      

        Early successes raised hopes that the machines of the time could not meet. Twice the money and the interest dried up. The methods that matter today were invented in these decades, mostly unnoticed.
      

      

        Chapter two: first steps and long winters. In 1958 Frank Rosenblatt presented the perceptron, a
        machine that learned from examples: the first neural network to
        become famous for it. Hopes ran high. In 1960 Herbert Simon predicted
        that within twenty years machines would do any work a person can. Five
        years later I. J. Good, who had worked beside Turing during the war, described an idea
        that still drives the debate about risk: a machine clever enough to
        design a better machine, which designs a better one again, until
        people are left far behind. A year later, at MIT, Joseph Weizenbaum
        wrote ELIZA, the first chatbot. It answered by rearranging what the
        user had typed. People confided in it all the same, which alarmed its
        author. But the computers of the time could not keep the promises made
        for them. In the 1970s the money dried up, and later it happened a

Timeline · 2003 to 2016

      
Deep learning arrives

      

        More data, faster graphics chips and a few stubborn researchers turn neural networks from a curiosity into the method that wins everything.
      

      

        Chapter three: deep learning arrives. The turn came slowly, and then all at once. In 2003
        Yoshua Bengio and his colleagues trained a neural network on language.
        It learned a list of numbers, a vector, for every word, and used them
        to predict the next one. Few people noticed. What neural networks
        lacked was data and computing power. By 2012 both had arrived. A deep
        network called AlexNet, trained on two graphics cards, won the leading
        contest in image recognition by a wide margin. The deep-learning boom
        began here, and so did the hunger for graphics chips that has not
        stopped since. The ideas now came quickly. In 2013 Google released
        word-to-vec, a fast way to learn word vectors, and one sum became
        famous: king, minus man, plus woman, lands close to queen. In 2014
        three researchers let a translation model look back at the words that
        mattered most for the word it was writing. They called it attention.
        In 2015 came distillation: a small network learning from a large one.
        Then, in 2016, the wider world took notice. DeepMind's AlphaGo
        defeated Lee Sedol, one of the best players of the game of Go, a
        decade earlier than most experts had expected. Unlike Deep Blue, this
        machine had learned. And in the same year Google Translate switched to
        a neural network. Its translations improved at a stroke, for hundreds
        of millions of people. Few of them knew that a neural network was now
        doing the work.

Timeline · 2017 to 2021

      
The transformer years

      

        One paper replaces the machinery of language models with attention alone. Within four years almost every idea in this guide exists, though few people outside research have noticed.
      

      

        Chapter four: the transformer years. In 2017 eight researchers at Google published a paper
        with a confident title: Attention Is All You Need. Until then,
        language models had read a sentence one word after another. The new
        design, the transformer, relied on attention alone and processed all
        the words at once. It could be trained far faster, on far more text.
        Every large language model since is built on it. OpenAI used it for a
        simple recipe: first train on enormous amounts of plain text, then
        adapt. The first GPT appeared in 2018. GPT-2, a year later, wrote
        whole coherent paragraphs, and its makers released it only in stages,
        out of concern about misuse. In 2020 came GPT-3, a hundred times
        larger, and able to do tasks it had never been trained for when shown
        a few examples. In 2019 the researcher Rich Sutton looked back on
        seventy years of the field and saw one pattern. Again and again,
        researchers built their own knowledge into their systems, and again
        and again they were overtaken by simpler methods that just used more
        computing power. He called it the bitter lesson: cleverness matters
        less than scale. Not everyone was persuaded. In 2021 a paper by Emily
        Bender, Timnit Gebru and colleagues argued that a language model
        stitches together words it has seen, with no reference to meaning. Its
        title gave the sceptical side of the debate a name: stochastic
        parrots. And yet the results kept coming. In 2020 DeepMind's AlphaFold
        2 predicted the three-dimensional shape of proteins about as
        accurately as laboratory experiments, a problem biologists had worked
        on for fifty years. In 2021 GitHub Copilot began to suggest lines of
        code as programmers typed, and programming became the first profession
        in which working alongside a model was routine. By the end of these
        years almost every idea in this guide existed. Few people outside
        research had noticed.

Questions · Everyday use

      
When you use a model

      

        Does it learn from my conversations, and is my data used for training?
        

          Not while you chat; the memory question further down explains why. But the company running it may store conversations and use them to train a later model. Whether it does depends on the product and your settings. Offers for businesses usually exclude this by default, and consumer apps often let you switch it off. The privacy settings of the product are the place to look.
        

        
Background: Using, step 5 · Training, step 1 · FAQ, memory

      

      

        Is it doing anything while I am not typing?
        

          No. A model computes only when it is called. Between your messages nothing runs: it does not wait, think things over or notice time passing. Each message starts a fresh run in which it reads the whole conversation again. The exception is an agent that some software has been told to start on a schedule.
        

        
Background: Using, step 9 · Agents, step 2

      

      

        Why can it not reliably count the letters in a word, or do exact arithmetic?
        

          Because it sees tokens, not letters. A word may arrive as one or two chunks, and the letters inside a chunk are never shown to it separately. Arithmetic has a similar cause: the model predicts the digits of the result as text, which works for common sums and fails for long ones. Both get much better when the model writes out its steps, and reliable when it hands the job to a tool such as a calculator.
        

        
Background: Using, step 2 · Using, step 9 · Agents, step 3

      

      

        Does a model know how it works inside?
        

          Mostly not. It cannot inspect its own weights. When you ask how it reached an answer, it writes a plausible account in the same way it writes everything else, and that account may or may not match what happened inside. What it says about itself comes from its training text and its system prompt. Finding out what really happens inside a model is a research field of its own, done from the outside.
        

        
Background: Using, step 7 · FAQ, under debate

      

      

        Why are models so good at programming, of all things?
        

          Several things come together. Code is text, and the internet holds
          an enormous amount of it in good quality, with explanations and
          worked answers alongside. It follows strict rules, which makes the
          next token easier to predict than in everyday language. Most
          important, code can be checked by a machine: it runs or it does
          not, and tests pass or fail. That gives training a clear signal
          for right and wrong without a person judging every answer, and it
          lets an agent try, test and fix until it works. And the companies
          building models put special effort here, because programmers are
          their own staff and their best customers.
        

        

          The limits are the usual ones. Models are strongest on tasks that
          resemble much-seen code, and they can produce code that looks
          right and is subtly wrong, so testing stays necessary.
        

        
Background: Training, step 1 · Training, step 5 · Agents, step 3 · Using, step 7

      

      

        What is vibe coding, and how does spec-driven development differ?
        

          Vibe coding is a term coined by the researcher Andrej
          Karpathy in 2025. You describe in plain language what you want, a
          coding agent writes the program, you try it out and say what to
          change, and you never read the code. It is fast, and it lets
          people build software who never learned to program. Its weakness
          is the other side of the same coin: nobody has checked what the
          code actually does, so mistakes, security holes and tangled
          structure pile up unnoticed. It suits prototypes and tools you
          can throw away.
        

        

          Spec-driven development, also called spec coding,
          turns the order round. First you write down, with the model's
          help, what the software must do, how it is to be built and how it
          will be tested. That document is the specification. The agent
          then works through it task by task, and what it produces is
          checked against it. This is slower to start and far more
          dependable for anything that has to last.
        

        

          The difference is where the human attention goes: on the result
          of each attempt, or on the description before any code exists.
          Many teams use both, vibes to explore an idea and a specification
          to build it properly.

Questions · Choosing and running

      
When you choose or run one

      

        Is a bigger model always better?
        

          No. The number of parameters says how much a model could hold, not how good it is. The training data, the training method and the post-training matter as much, which is why a small recent model often beats a far larger one from two years earlier. Bigger also means slower and more expensive. The sensible choice is the smallest model that does your task well.
        

        
Background: Using, step 5 · Training, step 6

      

      

        How do I read a model name such as "Example-3-27B-Instruct-Q4"?
        

          The name here is made up, but real ones follow the same pattern. First comes the family and its version. "27B" is the size: 27 billion parameters. "Instruct" or "Chat" means it has been post-trained to follow instructions, as opposed to "Base". "Q4" or "4bit" means it is quantized to about 4 bits per weight. Other common parts: "MoE" or "8x7B" for mixture of experts, "Vision" or "VL" for image input, a figure such as "1M" for the context window, and "GGUF" for a file format used to run models locally.
        

        
Background: Using, step 5 · Training, step 4 · Models, 3

      

      

        What hardware do I need to run a model myself?
        

          Above all, enough memory: the size of a model is its parameters times the bits per weight, and it needs spare room on top. A graphics card, or a computer whose processor and graphics share fast memory, makes it quick; without one it works, slowly. The Hardware tab lets you try which model fits which machine.

Questions · Beyond the technology

      
Wider questions

      

        What is the difference between an LLM and machine learning?
        

          They are not opposites. An LLM is one kind of machine learning,
          the way a sports car is one kind of car. The terms nest inside
          each other.
        

        

          
Artificial intelligence

          
The whole field: getting machines to do things that seem to need intelligence, by any method.

          
Machine learning

          
The part of it in which a program is not given rules but learns them from examples.

          
Deep learning

          
Machine learning with neural networks of many layers.

          
LLM

          
A very large deep-learning model trained on text.

        

        

          What people usually mean by the question is the difference from
          the machine learning that came before. A classic model is built
          for one job: a team collects examples with the right answers
          attached, such as emails marked "spam" or "not spam", and trains a
          small model that does exactly that and nothing else. An LLM is first trained on enormous amounts of text
          that nobody had to label, because the next word in the text is
          itself the right answer, and can then be pointed at almost any
          task by describing it in a prompt. Nothing has to be retrained to change the task.
        

        

          That generality has a price. An LLM is far larger and costlier to
          run, its output is free text that can be wrong in fluent ways, and
          it is harder to say why it answered as it did. Where the task is
          narrow and the data is a table of numbers, such as forecasting
          sales or spotting fraud, classic machine learning is often still
          the cheaper, faster and more accurate choice.
        

        
Background: Training, step 2 · Using, step 5 · Using, the different path

      

      

        How much energy does one answer use?
        

          For a short text answer, about a third of a watt-hour: Google reported 0.24 Wh for a typical Gemini text prompt in 2025, and OpenAI gave 0.34 Wh for an average ChatGPT query. That is roughly a small LED lamp burning for two minutes. Three cautions: these figures come from the companies themselves, they leave out the energy spent on training, and long reasoning, images and video use many times more. The total matters because of the sheer number of requests. Google's study · Altman's essay
        

        
Background: Using, step 9 · Training, step 2

      

      

        Can text written by a model be detected?
        

          Not reliably. Detection tools make mistakes in both directions, and they have wrongly accused people of using AI, so their verdict is not proof. Some providers build a hidden statistical pattern, a watermark, into what their models write. That only works for text from those providers, and editing the text weakens it.
        

        
Background: Using, step 8

      

      

        Who owns what a model writes, and was it allowed to learn from copyrighted text?
        

          Both questions are unsettled and are answered differently from country to country. Courts are still deciding whether training on copyrighted material is permitted and under what conditions. As for the output: in many places text made by a machine alone is not protected by copyright at all, and the terms of the provider say what you may do with it. For anything that matters, this needs proper legal advice.

Questions · Mind and machine

      
Is it like a mind?

      

        Words like neuron, memory and learning are borrowed from biology.
        Some of the comparisons help, some mislead.
      

      

        Does a model have a memory like ours?
        

          People keep a few things in mind for the moment and a lifetime of
          knowledge in the background. A language model has a rough
          counterpart to each, which is a useful way to remember what it can
          and cannot do.
        

          

            
              
In peopleIn a language modelWhere the comparison breaks

            
            
              

                Short-term memory. What you are holding in mind right now.
                The context window: everything in the current conversation.
                Yours is small and fades. The model's is large and holds the text word for word, although in a very long conversation it does not use every part equally well. It ends abruptly at the limit and is wiped when the conversation ends.
              

              

                Long-term memory. Knowledge and skills built up over years.
                The weights, shaped during training.
                You often sense whether you know something or are guessing, though human memory invents too. A model has at best a weak version of that sense: it cannot look anything up in its weights, and a plausible guess comes out looking exactly like a fact.
              

              

                Notes and diaries. Things you wrote down to read later.
                External memory: files, retrieved documents and the "memory" features of chat products, which save text and paste it back into later conversations.
                It is reading, not remembering. Whatever the software does not put back into the context window does not exist for the model.
              

              

                Learning from experience. Today's events become tomorrow's memories, partly during sleep.
                Nothing, until the next training run.
                A model in use does not learn from talking to you. Its weights are frozen.
              

            
          

        

          The practical upshot: a model does not remember you between
          conversations. If it seems to, some software saved a note about
          you and handed it back.
        

        
Background: Using, step 9 · Using, step 5 · Using, step 7

      

      

        Is a neural network like a brain?
        

          Neural networks were inspired by the brain, and the family
          resemblance is real: many simple units, connected by links of
          different strength, with learning as a change in those strengths
          and knowledge spread across the whole network. Beyond that, the
          two are very different machines.
        

          

            
              
The brainA language model

            
            
              

                A neuron is a living cell with complex behaviour of its own.
                An artificial "neuron" is one tiny calculation inside a layer (step 5 of the first tab): a weighted sum followed by a simple cut-off.
              

              

                About 86 billion neurons with something like 100 trillion connections.
                Billions to trillions of weights, which are far simpler than a connection in the brain.
              

              

                Learns all the time, from few examples, on about 20 watts.
                Trained in advance and then frozen, on more text than a person could read in a thousand lifetimes, in data centres that draw as much power as a town. Running a large finished model takes far less, but still hundreds of watts or more; only small models run on a phone's few watts.
              

              

                Lives in a body, with senses, needs and feelings.
                Receives text, and sometimes images or sound, and returns text.
              

              

                How it adjusts its connections is only partly understood.
                Uses backpropagation, which the brain is not known to use.
              

            
          

        

          Simplified: the figures for the brain are common estimates, and
          neuroscientists would add many qualifications to every row.

Still under debate

      

        The mechanics in this guide are well understood. What they add up to
        is not. On the following questions serious researchers disagree, and
        this guide does not take a side.
      

      

        Does it understand, or only predict?
        

          One view: it reproduces patterns of words without
          any grasp of what they refer to, a "stochastic parrot".
        

        

          Another view: predicting text this well is only
          possible with some internal model of the world the text
          describes, and that is a form of understanding.
        

        
Background: Using, step 7

      

      

        Can it reason, or does it imitate reasoning?
        

          One view: it recombines reasoning it has seen,
          and fails when a problem is truly new.
        

        

          Another view: it solves problems nobody wrote
          down before, and human reasoning also leans heavily on familiar
          patterns.
        

        
Background: Using, step 9

      

      

        Could such a system be conscious?
        

          One view: no. Consciousness belongs to living
          bodies with needs and something at stake, and calculation on text
          has none of that.
        

        

          Another view: if consciousness depends on the kind
          of processing and not on the material, a machine could in
          principle have it.
        

        

          Nobody knows which is true, and there is no agreed test.
        

        
Background: Using, step 5

      

      

        Will more of the same lead to human-level intelligence?
        

          One view: larger models, more data and better
          harnesses will get to AGI, artificial general
          intelligence, within years.
        

        

          Another view: something essential is missing,
          such as learning from experience, a body or real goals, and
          scaling will not supply it.
        

        
Background: Training, step 2

      

      

        Whose values should it follow, and who answers for what it does?
        

          The difficulty: training gives a model its
          character, so somebody chooses it. And when an agent acts, the
          responsibility is shared between its maker, whoever deployed it
          and whoever gave it the task, in ways the law has not settled.

Words you will hear

      

        
Agent
 
A model running in a loop with tools, so it can look things up and take actions over several rounds. Step 9

        
Agentic AI
 
Systems in which a model is given a goal and works towards it over many rounds, using tools. Agents, step 1

        
AGI
 
Artificial general intelligence: a system as capable as a person across most kinds of thinking work. Whether and when it will exist is disputed. FAQ

        
Alignment
 
Getting a model to reliably behave the way its makers intend. Training, step 5

        
API
 
Application programming interface: the way one program calls another, usually over the network. Made for programmers, not for models. Agents, step 4

        
Attention
 
The part of a layer where each token pulls in information from the tokens before it. Step 6

        
Attention head
 
One of the many attention patterns a layer runs side by side. Each can learn to look for something different. Step 6

        
Backpropagation
 
The method that works out, for every weight, which way to nudge it to reduce the loss. Training, step 3

        
Base model
 
A model after pre-training only: it continues text but is not yet an assistant. You will also hear "foundation model", a broader term for any large pre-trained model that others build on. Training, step 2

        
Benchmark
 
A fixed set of test questions with known answers, used to score and compare models. Training, step 6

        
Classifier
 
A model that does not write text but returns a probability for each of a fixed set of answers. Different path

        
CLI
 
Command-line interface: starting and controlling programs by typing text commands. Agents, step 4

        
Compaction
 
Replacing the older part of a long conversation with a summary, to free room in the context window. Details from that part are lost. Step 9

        
Context window
 
The maximum number of tokens a model can take in at once. Also used for the text currently in it. Step 9

        
CPU
 
Central processing unit: the ordinary processor found in every computer. Step 1

        
CUDA
 
NVIDIA's platform for programming its graphics chips. Most AI software was written for it first, which is a large part of NVIDIA's lead. Hardware, step 3

        
Deep learning
 
Machine learning with neural networks of many layers. LLMs are deep-learning models. FAQ

        
Diffusion model
 
A model that makes a picture, video or sound by starting from random noise and removing it step by step, guided by a description. Most picture and video generators work this way; LLMs do not. Models, 5

        
Distillation
 
Training a small model to imitate a large one. Training, step 7

        
Embedding
 
The vector a token ID is swapped for at the start. Also the name for that lookup. Step 4

        
External memory
 
Text saved outside the model, such as files or a product's "memory" feature, and placed back into the context window when needed. The model itself keeps nothing between conversations. FAQ

        
Fine-tuning
 
Training an already trained model further on a smaller, chosen set of examples: by its maker to turn a base model into an assistant, or later by others to specialise it. Training, step 4

        
Frontier model
 
One of the most capable models available at a given time. Models, 1

        
Generative
 
Describes a model that produces new text, token by token. The G in GPT. Different path

        
GPT
 
Generative pre-trained transformer: a model that generates text, was trained in advance, and is built as a transformer. Step 5

        
GPU
 
Graphics processing unit: a chip built to do huge numbers of simple calculations at once, first made for computer graphics. Neural networks run and train on them. Step 1

        
Gradient descent
 
Improving a model by moving its weights in small steps in the direction that lowers the loss. Training, step 3

        
Hallucination
 
Fluent, confident output that is wrong. The model scores what is likely, not what is true. Step 7

        
Harness
 
The software around a model that runs the loop: it builds the input, calls the model, carries out tool requests and enforces the rules. Agents, step 2

        
Humanoid
 
A robot with the shape of a person, meant to work in places built for people. Robots, step 1

        
Inference
 
Using a finished model to produce output. Nothing is learned; the weights stay fixed. Step 5

        
Input tokens
 
All the tokens a model reads for one answer: system prompt, conversation, documents and tool descriptions. Step 9

        
Knowledge cutoff
 
The date after which a model knows nothing, because its training data ends there. Training, step 1

        
Layer
 
One stage of the neural network. Each layer rewrites every vector a little. Step 5

        
Learning rate
 
A setting that scales how big each step of gradient descent is. Training, step 3

        
LLM
 
Large language model: a neural network trained on huge amounts of text to predict the next token. Step 1

        
LoRA
 
Low-rank adaptation: a cheap way to fine-tune, in which the original weights stay frozen and only a small add-on is trained. Training, step 7

        
Loss
 
A number for how wrong the model's guesses are. Training tries to make it smaller. Training, step 2

        
Machine learning
 
Getting a program to learn rules from examples instead of being given them. LLMs are one kind of machine learning. FAQ

        
MCP
 
Model Context Protocol: an open standard for connecting tools and data sources to any model's harness. Agents, step 4

        
Memory bandwidth
 
How fast a chip can move data in and out of its memory. When a model writes text, this often limits the speed more than calculating power does. Called reading speed in the Hardware tab. Hardware, step 1

        
Mixture of experts
 
A model design where each token is sent through only a few of many "expert" blocks. Often shortened to MoE. Step 6

        
MLX
 
Apple's open-source software for running and training models on its own chips, built to make use of their unified memory. The name is not an abbreviation with an official long form. Models, 6

        
Multimodal
 
Describes a model that takes more than text as input, such as images or sound. Step 4

        
Neural Engine
 
Apple's name for the NPU in its phones and Macs. Step 5

        
Neural network
 
The stack of layers that transforms the vectors. This is "the model". Step 5

        
Neuron
 
In a neural network: a tiny unit that multiplies its inputs by weights, adds them up and passes on the result. Loosely named after the nerve cells of the brain. Step 5

        
NPU
 
Neural processing unit: a small part of the main chip in recent phones and laptops, built to run modest models on the device itself. Step 5

        
Open weights
 
Describes a model whose weights are published, so anyone can download and run it. Not the same as open source. Step 5

        
Output tokens
 
The tokens a model writes. Each needs its own pass through the model, so they are slower and cost more than input tokens. Step 9

        
Parameters
 
Another word for weights. Model sizes are given as a parameter count. Step 5

        
Post-training
 
Everything done after pre-training to turn a base model into a helpful assistant. Training, step 4

        
Pre-training
 
The first and largest training phase: guessing the next token over enormous amounts of text. Training, step 2

        
Prompt
 
The text you give the model. Step 1

        
Prompt caching
 
Reusing the work already done on the unchanged beginning of an input, so it is processed faster and billed at a fraction of the price. It saves money and time, not room in the context window. Step 9

        
Prompt injection
 
Text in a web page, document or tool result, written to look like an instruction, that a model may follow. Agents, step 5

        
QPU
 
Quantum processing unit: the chip of a quantum computer. It plays no part in training or running today's language models. Step 5

        
Quantization
 
Storing each weight with fewer bits to make a model smaller and faster, at a small cost in quality. Step 5

        
RAG
 
Retrieval-augmented generation: a search step finds relevant text and places it in front of the prompt, so the model can answer from it. Step 7

        
Reasoning model
 
A model that first writes out its working, often hidden as "thinking", and then gives its answer. Step 9

        
Reinforcement learning
 
Learning from a score for what the model produced, instead of from a correct answer. Training, step 5

        
RLHF
 
Reinforcement learning from human feedback: people judge answers, and the model is trained towards the preferred ones. Training, step 5

        
Sampling
 
Picking the next token by a random draw, weighted by the probabilities. Step 8

        
Sovereign AI
 
Models that a country or region builds and runs itself, in its own data centres and under its own law, so as not to depend on companies abroad. In Europe mostly associated with Mistral AI. Models, 2

        
Spec-driven development
 
Writing a specification of what software must do before a coding agent builds it, then checking the result against it. Also called spec coding. FAQ

        
System One model
 
TypeSafe AI's name for its model Jev, which returns probabilities over predefined answers and writes no text. Different path

        
System prompt
 
Instructions that a chat product places in front of your prompt. You normally do not see them. Step 1

        
Teleoperation
 
Steering a robot by remote control. Recording it is the main way of collecting training data for robots. Robots, step 3

        
Temperature
 
A setting for how random the pick of the next token is. Step 8

        
Ternary
 
Weights limited to the three values −1, 0 and +1: the extreme end of quantization. Also called 1.58-bit. Step 5

        
Test-time training
 
Briefly training the model on the task in front of it while it answers, then usually discarding the changes. Short: TTT. Step 5

        
Token
 
A chunk of text, often a word or part of a word. The unit a model reads and writes. Step 2

        
Tokens per watt
 
How many tokens a machine writes each second for every watt of power it draws: a measure of energy efficiency. Higher is better. Hardware, step 6

        
Tool chain
 
Several tools used one after another, each result feeding the next request. Agents, step 3

        
Tool use
 
The model writes a request in a fixed format, software runs the tool and pastes the result back into the text. Also called function calling. Step 9

        
TPU
 
Tensor processing unit: Google's own chip for neural networks, doing the job a GPU does elsewhere. Step 5

        
Training
 
Adjusting the weights, over enormous amounts of text, until predictions improve. Step 5

        
Training data
 
The text a model learns from. Training, step 1

        
Transformer
 
The layer design used by today's LLMs, built around attention. Step 5

        
Unified memory
 
One pool of fast memory shared by the processor and the graphics part of a chip, as in Apple's Macs. It lets large models load without a separate graphics card. Hardware, step 4

        
Vector
 
A list of numbers that represents a token inside the model. Step 4

        
Vibe coding
 
Building software by describing what you want to a coding agent and judging only the result, without reading the code. FAQ

        
Vision-language-action model
 
A model that takes camera pictures and an instruction and produces robot movements. Built like an LLM and usually started from one. Short: VLA. Robots, step 2

        
Vocabulary
 
The fixed list of all tokens a model knows. Step 3

        
VRAM
 
Video random-access memory: the memory on a graphics card. It decides how large a model the card can hold. Step 5

        
Weights
 
The learned numbers that decide what each layer does. Fixed after training. Step 5
